# Story, script and performance

## Minimum useful story information

For narrative: protagonist, objective, obstacle, stakes, change and ending. For nonfiction: question/promise, ordered explanation or evidence, takeaway. For an ad: problem/desire, supportable benefit, demonstration/proof and desired action. Use the structure that fits; do not force a three-act screenplay onto a six-second logo animation.

Choose a treatment (plain-language account of what happens), beat outline (major changes) or full script based on stage. Clearly label voiceover, on-screen dialogue, text, visual action and sound so one is not mistaken for another.

## Script table

Use columns only as needed: `beat/time | visual action | dialogue/VO | on-screen text | sound | purpose`. Give each beat one primary function. Dialogue should communicate character intent, not narrate every visible action. Write copy for speaking and read it aloud or synthesize a draft only if authorized; use actual timing before final edit lock.

Do not equate a word count with exact duration. Mark estimates and account for pauses, reactions, breaths and visual reading time. For multiple speakers, assign stable speaker IDs and voice/performance notes. Avoid sending all speakers' identity images into an unrelated single-person shot.

## Performance direction

Describe intention and observable behavior: "trying to hide disappointment; brief hesitation before replying" is more actionable than "very emotional." Specify gaze target, physical action, gesture size, speech tempo and changes within the shot where needed. Avoid requesting numerous emotions and complex body actions in the same short generation.

For narrator-led work, agree tone, language/accent where relevant, pronunciation of names/technical terms and pace. For on-camera dialogue, plan lip-sync, voice continuity and how reaction/insert shots can cover difficult edits. Do not promise perfect native dialogue without inspecting a sample.

## Coverage and sound

Plan the images the editor needs to convey the turn. A shot can serve more than one purpose, but include readable starts/ends and suitable handles. Use sound to establish off-screen space, anticipate a transition or reveal emotion; it need not always duplicate what the image shows.

## Example beat

Scene purpose: a courier decides to help a stranger despite being late. Beat 1: clock and impatient foot establish urgency. Beat 2: dropped belongings interrupt the path. Beat 3: glance toward exit, then a choice to kneel and help. Beat 4: shared reaction completes the turn. The watch/hand action needs continuity; four dramatic camera moves do not.

## Script review

Check that the opening promises what the ending delivers, every required fact/copy is accurate, actions are physically intelligible, narration fits the planned time and the selected tool can plausibly produce the scene. If production is too difficult, offer a change preserving intent: simplify action, split the shot, use real footage, stage in Blender or cover with a motivated cut. Do not silently substitute still slides for requested action.
